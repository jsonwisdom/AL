# IN THE UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT

## Jane Doe, Plaintiff,

v.

## United States Department of Health and Human Services, Defendant.

Civil Action No. ___

# PLAINTIFF'S MEMORANDUM IN SUPPORT OF MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

## INTRODUCTION

This case does not ask the Court to ban automated adjudication. It asks the Court to apply familiar procedural due process principles to a benefits denial produced by an automated system whose decisional record cannot be meaningfully reviewed.

Plaintiff Jane Doe received Medicaid long-term care benefits based on disability and income eligibility. On March 12, 2026, the agency issued a denial and termination notice stating that an automated eligibility system had determined she no longer qualified. The notice did not identify the model or ruleset used, the policy version applied, the input provenance relied upon, the transformation chain from facts to denial, any output receipt hash, any audit log, or any replayable manifest by which Plaintiff, counsel, the agency, or this Court could verify what happened.

The agency says Plaintiff may appeal. But an appeal without a reviewable record is not meaningful review. If the state acts through a machine, due process requires a record sufficient to inspect the machine-mediated decision. Existing law already requires notice, an opportunity to be heard, and a record adequate for judicial review. Those requirements cannot be satisfied by a conclusory denial letter and an uninspectable automated process.

The Court should hold that when a materially consequential benefits decision is produced without a replayable manifest, the decision creates a rebuttable presumption of procedural due process violation. The practical remedy is remand for human review on a defined timeline, with the burden on the agency to produce a reviewable record and prove the process was lawful.

## FACTUAL BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Jane Doe is a disabled adult who received Medicaid long-term care benefits. Those benefits are a protected property interest once conferred under governing eligibility rules. On March 12, 2026, Plaintiff received a notice stating that her benefits would terminate because an automated eligibility review determined she no longer met program requirements.

The denial letter contained a generic explanation: "Based on available information, you do not meet eligibility criteria." It did not provide a plain-language explanation of the decisive facts or thresholds. It did not disclose the model identifier, ruleset, policy version, input provenance, or audit record. It did not state whether the automated system had changed since prior eligibility determinations. It did not provide a replayable manifest or any record permitting Plaintiff to test whether the system applied the correct policy to the correct information.

Plaintiff timely appealed and requested the administrative record. The record produced by the agency included the denial notice, a benefits summary, and several database screenshots. It did not include a manifest, policy hash, model identifier, transformation chain, output receipt hash, audit log, replay path, or plain-language explanation tied to the actual decision. The agency therefore cannot show what automated process was applied, whether the process was lawful, or whether the decision can be reproduced.

## QUESTION PRESENTED

Whether a federal agency violates procedural due process and acts arbitrarily and capriciously when it terminates a protected benefits interest through an automated system while failing to provide a replayable decisional record sufficient for meaningful notice, hearing, administrative review, and judicial review.

## STANDARD OF REVIEW

Summary judgment is appropriate where no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. In review of agency action, the Court determines whether the agency action was constitutional, in accordance with law, and not arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.

## ARGUMENT

### I. Procedural Due Process Requires Meaningful Review of the Process That Deprived Plaintiff of Benefits.

The Fifth Amendment prohibits the federal government from depriving a person of property without due process of law. Benefits once conferred under statutory criteria are not gratuities that may be terminated by opaque administrative assertion. They are protected interests requiring meaningful notice and an opportunity to be heard.

In *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970), the Supreme Court held that welfare benefits could not be terminated without procedures adequate to protect recipients from erroneous deprivation. In *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), the Court established the familiar balancing test: the private interest affected, the risk of erroneous deprivation and value of additional safeguards, and the government's interest.

Those principles do not weaken when the government uses software. They become more important. An automated system can deny benefits faster than a human can understand, appeal, or reconstruct the record. If the record is not preserved at the level of the computation, traditional review becomes a formality.

Here, Plaintiff's private interest is substantial: continued access to medical and long-term care benefits. The risk of erroneous deprivation is high because the agency cannot produce a reviewable record of the automated decision. The value of additional safeguards is obvious: a replayable manifest identifying the policy version, inputs, transformation chain, model or ruleset, output receipt, and audit trail. The government's interest in efficiency cannot justify a process that cannot be inspected.

### II. The Agency Cannot Carry Its Burden Without a Replayable Manifest.

Due process requires more than a conclusion. A person must receive notice sufficient to understand the basis for the action and an opportunity to contest it. A court must have a record sufficient to review the agency's action.

The agency's record here is missing the minimum elements needed to test the automated denial:

- model or ruleset identifier;
- policy or version identifier;
- timestamp;
- input provenance classification;
- transformation chain;
- output receipt hash;
- audit log;
- plain-language explanation tied to the actual decision; and
- replay path sufficient for independent review.

Without those elements, Plaintiff cannot know what to contest. The agency cannot show it applied the correct rule. And the Court cannot determine whether the decision was lawful.

This is not a demand for source code in every case. It is a demand for a reviewable decisional record. The agency may protect legitimate security or trade-secret interests, but it may not eliminate constitutional review by delegating the operative decision to an uninspectable process.

### III. Under Mathews, Machine-Speed Adjudication Requires Machine-Resolution Records.

The *Mathews* framework is practical. It asks what process is due in context. The context here is automated adjudication, where evidence may be generated, transformed, scored, and discarded at machine speed.

A paper summary cannot substitute for the missing computational record. If the agency acts at machine speed, affected persons must be able to trigger verification at machine speed. Otherwise, the state enjoys the efficiency of automation while the citizen receives only the delay of traditional appeal.

The additional safeguard Plaintiff seeks is modest: require the agency to produce a replayable manifest or accept a rebuttable presumption that the automated decision violated due process. This safeguard directly reduces erroneous deprivation and imposes only the burden of preserving the record the agency should already maintain if it chooses to automate protected-interest decisions.

### IV. Goldberg Supports Pre-Deprivation or Prompt Post-Deprivation Review With a Meaningful Record.

*Goldberg* recognizes that termination of subsistence benefits can inflict immediate and severe harm. A hearing is meaningful only if the recipient can understand and contest the basis of termination.

Here, Plaintiff could not meaningfully contest the denial because the agency provided no reviewable explanation of the machine-mediated process. A hearing officer reviewing screenshots and a conclusory denial letter cannot test whether the model or ruleset applied the correct policy, used the correct data, or drifted from the agency's own eligibility rules.

For benefits, housing, medical access, and similar protected interests, the agency must provide the record necessary for meaningful review before termination or within a prompt defined period sufficient to prevent irreparable harm.

### V. Overton Park and the APA Require a Reviewable Administrative Record, Not an Opaque Result.

In *Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe*, 401 U.S. 402 (1971), the Supreme Court required searching review based on the full administrative record. Courts cannot accept post hoc rationalizations or conclusory assertions of agency regularity when the record does not reveal the basis for action.

The same principle applies here. The automated system is not outside the administrative record. It is part of the administrative record. When software performs the operative analysis, the record must include enough information to review that analysis.

Under the Administrative Procedure Act, agency action that cannot be explained or reviewed is arbitrary and capricious. A benefits termination based on undisclosed model logic, missing policy versioning, absent input provenance, and no replay path prevents the Court from determining whether the agency considered the relevant factors or applied the law correctly.

### VI. The Court Should Adopt a Practical Rebuttable Presumption, Not a Ban on Automation.

Plaintiff does not ask the Court to ban automated systems. The requested rule is narrow:

When a materially consequential automated decision affects a protected liberty or property interest and the agency cannot produce a replayable manifest sufficient for meaningful review, the decision creates a rebuttable presumption of procedural violation.

The agency may rebut the presumption by producing a record sufficient to establish:

1. the applicable policy or rule version;
2. the model or ruleset applied;
3. the inputs or input categories used;
4. the transformation chain from inputs to output;
5. the output receipt or equivalent integrity record;
6. any relevant drift or policy changes;
7. a plain-language explanation understandable to the affected person; and
8. a replay path sufficient for independent review.

This standard is the logical extension of existing due process doctrine. It does not create a new constitutional right. It applies notice, hearing, administrative record, and meaningful review requirements to the actual process the government chose to use.

### VII. Execution Fidelity and Substantive Legitimacy Are Distinct.

A replayable record proves only that the system did what the record says it did. It does not prove that the governing rule was lawful, fair, or constitutional. The Court should preserve this distinction.

Track One asks: did the automated system execute as recorded?

Track Two asks: was the system allowed to do that at all?

The present motion can be resolved on Track One. The agency cannot even show execution fidelity because it lacks a replayable manifest. If the agency later produces a replayable record, Plaintiff may still challenge the substantive legitimacy of the model design, threshold, feature use, or policy logic. A valid hash does not cure an invalid rule.

### VIII. Delay That Functions as Denial Is Also Reviewable.

The agency may not cure an unreviewable automated denial by placing Plaintiff into an indefinite appeal queue. Where a routing or triage system determines whether a person receives review in days or years, that routing is materially consequential. A delay that functions as denial is itself a decision requiring due process.

If the agency cannot provide prompt human review because its automated system lacks a replayable record, the appropriate remedy is not indefinite backlog. The remedy is defined remand, burden-shifting to the agency, and restoration or preservation of benefits where necessary to prevent erroneous deprivation.

## REQUESTED RELIEF

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court:

1. grant summary judgment for Plaintiff;
2. hold that the agency's automated termination decision violated procedural due process and was arbitrary and capricious because the agency failed to provide a reviewable decisional record;
3. adopt a rebuttable presumption that materially consequential automated decisions affecting protected interests are procedurally defective when issued without a replayable manifest;
4. remand to the agency for human review within 30 days;
5. require the agency to produce the policy version, model or ruleset identifier, input provenance, transformation chain, audit log, output integrity record, and plain-language explanation used in Plaintiff's denial;
6. place the burden on the agency to prove that the denial was correct and lawfully produced; and
7. order interim preservation or restoration of benefits as necessary to prevent irreparable harm pending review.

## CONCLUSION

The Constitution does not forbid the government from using machines. It forbids the government from using machines to make protected-interest decisions beyond meaningful review.

This Court need not decide the future of artificial intelligence. It need only apply existing due process law to the record before it. A denial that cannot be replayed cannot be meaningfully reviewed. A process that cannot be inspected cannot carry the government's burden. And an appeal that lacks the record of the decision being appealed is not due process.

The motion should be granted.

## Appendix A: Proposed Judicial Rule

A materially consequential automated decision affecting a protected liberty or property interest is presumptively procedurally defective when the agency cannot produce a replayable manifest sufficient for meaningful notice, hearing, administrative review, and judicial review.

The presumption may be rebutted only by a record showing the policy version, model or ruleset applied, input provenance, transformation chain, output integrity record, audit history, plain-language explanation, and replay path sufficient for independent review.

## Appendix B: Model Order Language

The Court holds that the agency's automated benefits termination violated procedural due process because the agency failed to preserve and produce a reviewable record of the automated decision. The matter is remanded for human review within 30 days. The agency bears the burden of producing the applicable policy version, model or ruleset identifier, input provenance record, transformation chain, output integrity record, audit log, and plain-language explanation tied to the challenged decision. Pending review, the agency shall preserve or restore benefits as necessary to prevent erroneous deprivation.
