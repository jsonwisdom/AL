# SSRN Abstract and Law Review Cover Letter

## Article Title

**Due Process at Machine Speed: The Constitutional Requirement for Replayable Administrative Records**

## SSRN Abstract

The administrative state increasingly uses automated and semi-automated systems to determine or materially influence benefits, eligibility, enforcement, immigration, healthcare access, housing, credit, and other protected interests. Existing procedural due process doctrine assumes that the basis of a government decision can be reconstructed from a human decision-maker's file, reasoning, testimony, notes, or explanation. Machine-speed adjudication breaks that assumption when agencies preserve only inputs and outputs while omitting the computational path that transformed one into the other.

This Article argues that the Fifth Amendment requires a replayable administrative record when government delegates materially consequential decisions to automated systems. This is not a new AI right and not a technology-policy proposal. It is the application of existing constitutional procedure to machine-mediated public power. Mathews v. Eldridge supplies the balancing framework. Goldberg v. Kelly establishes the need for meaningful process before serious benefits harm. Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe requires a reviewable administrative record. Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm requires reasoned decisionmaking. Together, these doctrines require notice, a meaningful opportunity to be heard, and a record sufficient to test the basis of agency action.

The Article develops two related concepts. First, speed parity: when the state acts at machine speed, the affected person must be able to trigger verification at machine speed. Second, the two-track framework: execution fidelity asks whether the system did what the record says it did, while substantive legitimacy asks whether the system was allowed to do that at all. A replayable record proves execution; it does not prove justice.

The Article proposes a modest judicial rule: a materially consequential automated decision affecting protected interests is presumptively procedurally defective when the agency cannot produce a replayable decisional record. The agency may choose its own implementation. The Constitution sets the floor: no manifest, no meaningful review.

# Law Review Cover Letter

Dear Articles Editors,

I am pleased to submit **Due Process at Machine Speed: The Constitutional Requirement for Replayable Administrative Records** for your review.

This Article addresses a problem that is already reaching courts and agencies: government decisions affecting benefits, eligibility, enforcement, healthcare access, immigration, housing, and other protected interests are increasingly made or materially shaped by automated systems, yet the administrative records produced in those cases often contain only the inputs and output—not the computational path that transformed one into the other.

The Article's central claim is deliberately doctrinal, not speculative: this is not an AI ethics argument, a responsible-innovation proposal, or a request for courts to create new rights. It is a procedural due process argument under existing law. Mathews v. Eldridge, Goldberg v. Kelly, Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe, Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm, and the APA already require meaningful notice, an opportunity to be heard, reasoned decisionmaking, and a reviewable record. The Article asks what those requirements mean when the operative decision path is computational.

The answer is a replayable administrative record. When an agency delegates a materially consequential decision to an automated or semi-automated system, the computation is part of the decisional record. A denial letter is not the record; it is a summary of the result. Without a replayable record, the affected person cannot know what to contest, the agency cannot prove what happened, and the court cannot conduct meaningful review.

The Article offers a court-ready standard that does not require judges to become systems architects: a materially consequential automated decision affecting a protected interest is presumptively procedurally defective when the agency cannot produce a replayable decisional record. Agencies remain free to choose how they comply. The constitutional floor is reviewability.

The piece is timely because automated adjudication is no longer theoretical. Agencies and contractors are already deploying systems that affect benefits, housing, healthcare access, immigration processing, regulatory enforcement, and public eligibility. Litigation is following, but courts lack a concise doctrinal framework for translating existing due process principles into machine-mediated adjudication. This Article provides that framework.

The practical contribution is the Article's two-track model. Execution fidelity asks whether the system did what the record says it did. Substantive legitimacy asks whether the system was allowed to do that at all. This distinction prevents courts from confusing a technically replayable output with a lawful or just one. Replay proves execution; it does not prove justice.
